Development and Psychometric Evaluation of a Bengali Illness Anxiety Disorder Scale

Sanjida Alom Borsa, Al Amin, Masom Miah

The question

Illness anxiety means a persistent fear of having a serious illness. Some people respond by checking and seeking reassurance from doctors again and again; others avoid care because they fear what they might hear. We set out to build a Bengali instrument that captures both patterns, and to test whether it measures them reliably.

What we did

We drafted 21 items across four proposed domains: preoccupation and fear, care-seeking and checking, care avoidance, and functional impairment. A panel of psychologists, an English-language expert, and a psychiatrist translated and back-translated the items, and 37 participants tested them in cognitive interviews. We then surveyed 480 university students, used one half of the sample to explore the scale's structure and the other half to confirm it, and checked the scores against anxiety, depression, and self-reported doctor visits.

What we found

The scale was highly reliable (α = .92), and the four-domain model fit the validation half well. Scores tracked generalized anxiety closely (r = .75), and care-seeking scores tracked how often students reported seeing a doctor in the past six months. One result was not clean: parallel analysis favored two components rather than four, so the scale's dimensionality still needs work.

Why it matters to me

Many screening tools used in Bangladesh were developed in other languages and contexts. This project made a question concrete for me: what evidence does a tool need before it can inform decisions about people whose language and context differ from where it was developed? I want to bring that question to school assessment.

Read the abstract

Background. Illness anxiety involves persistent concern about serious illness and may show up as repeated reassurance seeking or as avoidance of medical care. This study developed a 21-item Bengali illness anxiety disorder scale and evaluated its measurement properties among university students in Bangladesh.

Methods. Cross-sectional study of 480 university students. Analyses included item statistics, Cronbach's alpha, Pearson correlations, exploratory parallel analysis in a calibration half (n = 240), and maximum-likelihood confirmatory factor analysis in a validation half (n = 240).

Results. Internal consistency was high (α = .921; domains .778–.858). The four-factor model fit well (χ²(183) = 270.78, CFI = .956, TLI = .949, RMSEA = .045), although parallel analysis favored two components. Total scores correlated with generalized anxiety (r = .751) and depressive symptoms (r = .552). Care-seeking correlated with six-month physician visits (r = .473), and care avoidance correlated with functional impairment (r = .704).

Conclusions. The scale showed high internal consistency and associations consistent with illness-anxiety-related distress and behavior. Evidence on dimensionality was mixed; ordinal factor analysis, measurement invariance testing, and validation against clinical assessment are needed before diagnostic screening use.

Under Pressure to Succeed: Self-Compassion, Academic Expectations Stress, and Anxiety in Bangladeshi University Admission Candidates

Sanjida Alom Borsa, Atashi Proshad

The question

In Bangladesh, the months before university admission exams are some of the most pressured in a student's life. Candidates carry their own expectations and those of parents and teachers. If a student treats a setback with kindness instead of harsh self-judgment, does the same pressure produce less anxiety? In other words, does self-compassion act as a buffer?

What we did

We gave paper questionnaires to 200 first-time admission candidates at an admission coaching centre in Dhaka. The questionnaires measured academic expectations stress, self-compassion, and anxiety symptoms. To test buffering, we fit a moderation model with centered predictors and robust (HC3) standard errors, then checked whether the result held after adjusting for demographics and removing the most influential case.

What we found

Students under more expectations stress reported more anxiety (r = .36), and more self-compassionate students reported less (r = −.42). However, the buffering effect was not supported: the interaction was small and not significant (p = .13). When the single most influential case was removed, the interaction ran in the opposite direction to buffering. We report this openly rather than overstating the finding.

Why it matters to me

Protective factors are only useful in schools if they actually protect. This study taught me to separate "is associated with less distress" from "changes how stress affects distress." That distinction matters when deciding which supports to offer students.

Read the abstract

Background. Academic expectations may accompany anxiety during preparation for university admission. This study examined whether self-compassion moderated the association between academic expectations stress and anxiety symptoms among Bangladeshi admission candidates.

Methods. A paper-based cross-sectional survey of 200 first-time university admission candidates aged 18 or older, using the Academic Expectations Stress Inventory (AESI), a local 24-item self-compassion questionnaire, and the 7-item DASS-21 anxiety subscale. Moderation models used centered predictors and HC3 robust standard errors; sensitivity analyses adjusted for demographics and examined the most influential observation.

Results. Stress correlated positively with anxiety (r = .356) and self-compassion negatively (r = −.419), both p < .001. The full-sample interaction was not statistically supported (b = 0.405, 95% CI −0.114 to 0.924, p = .126; ΔR² = .0095). Excluding the most influential observation yielded a positive interaction (p = .045), indicating sensitivity.

Conclusions. Expectations stress and self-compassion were each associated with anxiety, but the primary analysis did not establish moderation, and the direction of the sensitivity result was opposite to the buffering hypothesis. Cross-sectional measurement and limited reliability of two self-compassion domains constrain interpretation.

Paradoxical Connectivity: The Lack of Correlation Between Internet Addiction and Mental Health Among Secondary Students in Dhaka

Sanjida Alom Borsa · supervised by Saudia Ferdaus

The question

A common assumption is that heavy internet use crowds out healthy activities and drives adolescents' psychological distress. Studies elsewhere often find exactly that. Does it hold for secondary school students in urban Dhaka, where the internet is now essential for school itself?

What I did

I designed the study, obtained permission from schools, and collected written consent from students and school authorities. I surveyed 100 students (50 boys, 50 girls) using Bengali versions of the Internet Addiction Test and the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12), then tested three hypotheses about how internet addiction, distress, and gender relate.

What I found

All three hypotheses were rejected. Internet addiction and psychological distress were essentially unrelated (r = −.03). Boys and girls had similar internet addiction scores, but boys reported significantly more distress. That points to pressures such as academic and career expectations placed on boys in South Asian families, and to norms that discourage boys from seeking emotional support.

Why it matters to me

Interpreting a null result taught me to respect the evidence and to ask better questions. What exactly are students doing online? How well do our measures capture it? Whose perspective is missing? These questions shape my interest in multi-informant, longitudinal research.

Read the abstract

This study investigates the relationship between digital dependency and psychological well-being among secondary school students in Dhaka, Bangladesh. A sample of 100 students (50 male, 50 female) completed the Bangla versions of the Internet Addiction Test (IAT) and the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12). Internet addiction and mental health showed a negligible correlation (r = −.0275), and internet addiction did not differ significantly by gender (t = 1.57, p > .05). General mental health scores did differ (t = 2.13, p < .05), with male students reporting more distress. These results challenge the assumption that internet addiction is the primary driver of psychological health in this population and suggest that other, gender-specific stressors, such as academic and career pressure, may be at play.

Next

What I want to study next

Together, these studies point to two doctoral questions. How can assessment distinguish students' learning needs from their emotional support needs? And what helps schools turn that information into timely support? I want to answer them with designs that combine student, teacher, and family reports, follow students over time, and produce Bengali-language tools that schools in Bangladesh can use.
